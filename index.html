Intelevents AI

I designed and built the entire platform, end to end: the marketing site and sales pages, the exhibitor dashboards, lead tables and AI ranking, a pipeline where every lead keeps its email, meeting and status history, CRM integrations, email and calendar booking, and more.

inteleventsai.com

The Intelevents AI homepage, which I designed and built.

The brief

Turn a pile of badge scans into a call list a sales team will trust.

Exhibitors leave a three-day show with hundreds of scans and no idea who matters. The AI could research and rank every lead. My job was to design and build everything around it, so people would understand the ranking, act on it, and never fight the tool on a loud show floor.

What I designed and built
  • Marketing siteLanding, sales and solutions pages
  • Exhibitor dashboardsLeads per day, sources and qualification rate, live during the show
  • Lead tablesSearch, sorting and the AI rank column
  • AI rankingScores, reasons, company research and competitor flags
  • PipelineA kanban board where every lead carries its email, meeting and status history
  • Mobile scanningTwo-tap qualifying that works offline
  • CRM integrationsSalesforce, HubSpot, Dynamics, Pipedrive and Zoho field mapping
  • Email and calendarGmail, Outlook, Google Calendar and Calendly, logged per lead
Design decisions

Four calls that shaped the product.

Make the AI explain itself

A bare number doesn't earn trust from a sales team. I designed every rank to ship with one plain sentence explaining it, and the same reason follows the lead into the CRM.

I also put the company profile the AI scores against in front of the user. When a ranking looks wrong, they can see why and fix the input.

Design for the show floor

Booth staff qualify a lead in seconds, one-handed, mid-conversation. Capture is two taps on large chips, with a notes field for anything else.

Hall Wi-Fi fails all the time, so offline is a designed state, not an error. The screen says "Saved on this device" and the lead syncs when the connection comes back.

Ranked, not filtered

The AI never hides a lead. Low scores and competitors sink to the bottom with a visible label, so a person can always overrule the model.

Lead cards on the pipeline board carry the rank too, so the order survives from the table to the kanban board.

A pipeline with memory

I built a kanban pipeline for the leads a team is actively working. Every card keeps its own history: status changes, emails sent from Gmail or Outlook, and booked meetings, all in one timeline.

Emails go from the user's own address and meetings land on their real calendar, so nothing feels like it came from a bot.

LastSet

A strength-training companion that remembers your last set and tells you exactly what to lift next.

SwiftUISwiftDataLive ActivitiesHealthKitIn TestFlight
The problem

Getting stronger is simple. Remembering is the hard part.

Progressive overload means doing a little more than last time. That only works if you know exactly what last time was, mid-workout, with chalk on your hands.

  • Last time, always on screen

    Every set shows what you did last session next to today's target. No digging through history.

  • Programs that don't care what day it is

    Miss a Tuesday and the program waits. Rotation follows your sessions, not the calendar.

  • Recommendations with a reason

    When a workout ends, each exercise gets a Next time target and a plain explanation of why.

The engine

Progress is a rule you can read.

LastSet uses double progression. Work inside a rep range, and once every set reaches the top of it, add weight. Try it below. It runs the same logic as the app.

Barbell Bench Press

3 sets · 8–10 reps · +5 lb

175 lb
TrainingEngines.swiftPure · unit tested
enum ProgressionEngine {
  static func evaluate(repetitions: [Int],
                       prescribedSetCount: Int,
                       repMaximum: Int,
                       currentWeight: Double,
                       increment: Double) -> ProgressionResult {
    guard prescribedSetCount > 0, repMaximum > 0,
          currentWeight > 0, increment > 0 else {
      return ProgressionResult(isReady: false, nextWeight: nil)
    }
    let completed = repetitions.prefix(prescribedSetCount)
    let ready = completed.count == prescribedSetCount
      && completed.allSatisfy { $0 >= repMaximum }
    return ProgressionResult(isReady: ready,
      nextWeight: ready ? currentWeight + increment : nil)
  }
}

No network, no model, no surprises. The same inputs always give the same answer, which is what you want with a loaded bar over your chest.

Live Activities

Your phone stays in your pocket.

The workout lives on the Lock Screen and in the Dynamic Island. Log, rest, adjust and skip without unlocking.

  • One deadline, everywhere

    Rest is stored as an absolute deadline, so the app, Lock Screen and Dynamic Island never drift apart.

  • Idempotent commands

    Buttons send App Intents that are safe to repeat. A double tap can't log a set twice.

  • A clear state machine

    Every session moves through Log, Rest, Finish and Summary, and stale steps are rejected.

Under the hood

Local-first, and built to be trusted.

SwiftUISwiftDataActivityKitApp IntentsHealthKitSwift ChartsWatchConnectivity

The phone is the source of truth

Every set is written to SwiftData on the device. Logging never waits on a network, which matters in a concrete basement gym.

Trade-off: no multi-device sync in the first release. Backup comes next.

History can't be rewritten

Completed sessions are saved as self-contained snapshots. Editing a program later never changes what you actually lifted.

Trade-off: some duplicated data in exchange for a record you can trust.

Rules over models

Progression and personal records come from small, pure functions with unit tests, not a black box.

Trade-off: it won't adapt to a bad night's sleep. You still can.

Substitutions keep the truth

Swap barbell bench for dumbbells mid-workout and history records what you actually did, for one set or the rest of them.

Trade-off: progress charts follow the exercise you performed, not the one planned.

Budgy

A budget tracker with one Postgres data model behind both a web portal and an offline-first iPhone app, running on my own server.

Next.jsNestJSPostgresDrizzleSwiftTurborepoSelf-hosted
budgy.local

Interface mockup with sample data.

Why I built it

Bank apps show what happened. I wanted to plan what happens next.

I wanted budgets I set myself, imports from any bank's CSV export, and my financial data on a server I control.

  • Imports keep the original

    Every CSV row is stored raw before it's normalized, so a parser fix can re-run over old imports without asking the bank again.

  • Shared by design

    Every row belongs to a workspace, with row-level security in Postgres, so a household can share one budget safely.

  • Works with no signal

    The iPhone app writes locally with client-generated IDs, then pushes and pulls changes when it's back online.

Under the hood

One contract, three clients.

The OpenAPI spec is the source of truth. The web portal and the iPhone app both use clients generated from it, so they can't drift from the API.

budgy/
├─ apps/
│  ├─ api        NestJS, OpenAPI, CSV import, sync
│  ├─ web        Next.js App Router portal
│  └─ ios        Swift package, SQLite store
└─ packages/
   ├─ db         Drizzle schema + SQL migrations
   └─ api-contract  openapi.yaml, generated types

Contract first

Endpoints are designed in the spec, then implemented. Both clients get typed access for free.

Trade-off: quick experiments take an extra step through the contract.

Postgres is the source of truth

The phone keeps a local SQLite copy for speed and offline use, and the server decides what's canonical.

Trade-off: sync conflicts need explicit rules instead of being ignored.

Tesla Inventory Tracker

It watched Tesla's inventory for the exact car I wanted and emailed me the minute one went live near me. I used it to buy my own car, and it saved me over $30,000.

Next.jsTypeScriptVercel CronEmail alertsHeadless ChromeSelf-hosted
inventory.local

Interface mockup with sample data.

The story

It found me a nearly new car for $30,000 less.

Used Teslas were being listed and sold within hours. With alerts set for the trims and prices I wanted, I got an email the moment the right car went live: a Model 3 with 5,000 km, every software unlock, and paid Full Self-Driving included. Tesla has since stopped selling FSD outright. Today it's a $99 monthly subscription.

$30k+saved compared with buying new
5,000 kmon the odometer, so basically new
$11kFull Self-Driving, already paid for and included. Now it's $99 a month.
Why I built it

In a fast market, the first email wins.

Tesla updates its inventory constantly, and the good cars don't last. Refreshing the site by hand means finding out after someone else already bought the car.

  • Alerts on your terms

    Choose new or used, the model, specific trims, a maximum price and a location. The tracker emails you as soon as a match goes live.

  • Every poll is kept

    Snapshots, config and poll state are stored on disk, so the dashboard can compare any day with any other.

  • Tested without touching Tesla

    Unit tests run offline by default. Live smoke tests only run when I opt in with a flag.

Under the hood

Polite by default.

The tracker treats someone else's API as a shared resource. It asks rarely, and it backs off when told to.

A floor on frequency

Hourly polling by default, with a 30-minute minimum enforced on the server so a settings mistake can't hammer the API.

Trade-off: a new listing can show up to an hour late.

Cooldown on rate limits

A 429 response puts the tracker into cooldown. It records when it's allowed to try again and skips polls until then.

Trade-off: gaps in the history during cooldowns.

Two fetch strategies, one interface

A direct request or a headless browser session, chosen in settings and returning the same snapshot shape.

Trade-off: browser mode is heavier to run.

JSON files over a database

Snapshots, config and state live in plain JSON. Easy to inspect, back up and diff.

Trade-off: fine for thousands of snapshots, not millions.

CGM Display Hub

I wanted more ways to see my blood sugar than the app on my phone. So I built a hub that pulls my readings and streams them to my Mac's menu bar and to small Arduino displays at work and at home.

Next.jsTypeScriptSupabase PostgresDexcom APISwiftUIArduinoSelf-hosted
cgm.local

Interface mockup with sample data. The hub is an informational display and is not used for dosing decisions.

Why I built it

My blood sugar, without reaching for my phone.

Checking a CGM normally means unlocking a phone many times a day. I wanted the number wherever I already am: on my Mac while I work, on my desk at the office, and on the wall in my bedroom.

  • Sync on a schedule

    A cron job pulls new readings from Dexcom every five minutes and stores them in Postgres.

  • One small, read-only API

    Two endpoints, latest and recent, normalized so any client can draw a reading in either mmol/L or mg/dL.

  • Screens everywhere

    A native macOS menu bar app, plus Arduino-powered mini displays in my office at work and on my bedroom wall.

The screens

Three screens, one number.

Each one reads the same API, so they always agree, and each one knows when a reading is too old to show.

Under the hood

A display API that refuses to lie.

GET /api/glucose/latestSample response
{
  "latest": {
    "value": 6.4,
    "unit": "mmol/L",
    "trend": "flat",
    "ageMinutes": 2,
    "stale": false,
    "source": "dexcom"
  },
  "delta": 0.1,
  "connected": true,
  "staleAfterMinutes": 15
}

Every reading says how old it is and whether it's stale, so a display can't present an old number as current.

Tokens never leave the server

Dexcom OAuth tokens are stored and refreshed server-side. Displays only ever see a separate read-only token.

Trade-off: the server is one more thing to keep running.

Built for tiny clients

The display token works as a header or a query parameter, because a small Arduino display can't always set headers easily.

Trade-off: query tokens can end up in logs, which is acceptable only because the token is read-only.
